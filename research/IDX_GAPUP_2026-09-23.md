# IDX menu 29f — what happens after a gap UP? — written 2026-09-23

The fade assumes the opening auction overreacts. If so it should overreact to good news too. Same data as menu 29d
(Yahoo cache 2015-2026, survivors, corporate actions removed); 8 trials, cumulative 558.

Reference — the deployed rule, gap <= -7 % bought at the open: 642 events, mean **+364 bps**, median +246, hit 60 %, daily t 8.6.

## Reading

- Worth a follow-up: fade_up >= +5 %, fade_up >= +7 %, fade_up >= +10 %.
- `fade_up` is charged two ticks and both fees but **no borrow cost**, and it is not executable by this account:
  IDX allows short selling only through a margin facility on an eligible list, which the desk does not have. It is
  measured to learn whether the overreaction is symmetric, not because it could be traded.
- If buying gap ups loses and fading them wins by about the same amount, the asymmetry is not an edge one can
  reach; if both lose, the opening auction is not systematically wrong about good news at all.

## Verdict written after the run

**The overreaction is symmetric, and it is on the wrong side of the account.**

- **Buying a gap up is the worst trade in this whole family**: -308 bps at +3 %, -433 at +5 %, -524 at +7 %, **-688 at
  +10 %**, hit rate 22-24 %, daily t -11 to -24, and negative in both auto-rejection regimes. Deeper gap up, bigger loss,
  monotone - the exact mirror of the fade, where deeper gap down means bigger gain. "Gap and go" on IDX is a way to lose
  money with great consistency, and this now says so with 5,891 events over twelve years, not menu 29's one arm.
- **Selling a gap up pays about what fading a gap down pays**: +314 bps at +7 % against the deployed rule's +364, with
  the same shape (hit 61 %, monotone in depth, positive in both eras, t 6.6 before and 4.7 after). The auction
  overreacts to good news as reliably as to bad.

Three of the eight arms clear the follow-up bar and **none of them can be traded here**: short selling on IDX runs
through a margin facility on the exchange's eligible-securities list, which this desk does not have, and the figures
above carry no borrow cost, so the real number would be lower still. The finding is worth recording for two reasons
anyway: it is independent evidence that the opening-auction overreaction is real (the fade is not a quirk of falling
prices), and it says plainly what NOT to do - never buy a gap up at the open.

The one executable use: a name in a book that gaps up 7 % or more at the open is, on this evidence, more likely to give
it back than to run. That belongs to the exit side of the value and trend books, not here, and it is not tested - it
would need its own study on names actually held.
